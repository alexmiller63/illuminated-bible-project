# Notes

¹ **Mo'adim.** The WEB renders mo'adim as “seasons.” The Hebrew has a broader range that includes appointed times, appointed occasions, or fixed times. In Genesis 1:14 the heavenly lights are associated with signs, mo'adim, days, and years.

² **Astronomical context.** Genesis 1:14 belongs to an ancient Near Eastern world in which celestial observation was important for calendars, appointed times, omens, and religious practice. Babylonian and other Mesopotamian traditions gave great significance to celestial phenomena. Genesis likewise assigns the heavenly lights functions involving signs, appointed times, days, and years, while presenting them as created objects under Elohim rather than as independent deities. This comparison supplies historical context; it does not by itself establish literary dependence on a particular Babylonian text.

³ **The greater light and the lesser light.** Biblical Hebrew has ordinary words for the sun, shemesh, and the moon, yareach. Genesis 1:16 does not use those names, instead describing them as the “greater light” and the “lesser light.” The stars, by contrast, are called by the ordinary word kokhavim. The reason for this difference is interpretive. One hypothesis is that the wording contributes to Genesis's presentation of the sun and moon as created objects rather than deities. Compare Deuteronomy 4:19, which within the Torah explicitly names the sun, moon, and stars while warning against worshiping them. Compare also Psalm 136:7–9.

⁴ **Raqia and shamayim.** Genesis 1:8 says that God called the raqia shamayim. Yet Genesis 1:14–17 subsequently uses the construction birqia hashamayim, “in the expanse of the heavens.” Raqia and shamayim therefore overlap closely but should not automatically be treated as perfectly interchangeable synonyms. The illumination raises the question of what relationship Genesis intends between the named realm shamayim and the raqia associated with it; it does not provide an answer.

# Earlier Note Revision

**Yom echad.** Genesis 1:5 calls the first day yom echad, “one day” or “day one.” Later numbered days use ordinal forms: compare yom sheni, “second day,” in Genesis 1:8 and yom shelishi, “third day,” in Genesis 1:13. The reason for the distinctive yom echad construction is uncertain.

# Illumination Inquiry Principle

Illumination may raise questions the text itself does not answer. Such questions are identified as hypotheses or inquiries rather than conclusions.
