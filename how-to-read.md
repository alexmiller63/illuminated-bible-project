# How to Read This Book

The Illuminated Bible is designed to be read at more than one level. You can simply read the biblical text, or you can stop wherever something catches your attention and explore the language beneath the translation.

**The right column is for reading. The left column is for illuminating what catches your attention.**

---

## How to Read the Cover

The cover begins the illumination before the first page is turned.

### Two Ancient Languages

The title of the biblical book appears in two ancient languages: Hebrew and Greek. They represent two different but closely connected parts of the history through which Genesis has come down to us.

### The Hebrew Title

The Hebrew title is written without vowel points. This is deliberate. A traditional Torah scroll writes the Hebrew consonantal text without the system of vowel points familiar from printed Hebrew Bibles. The cover echoes that appearance.

It should not, however, be mistaken for a reproduction of a Torah scroll. A traditional Torah scroll does not place a book title on Genesis in this fashion. The title is a modern element of the design. Writing it without vowel points is an acknowledgment of the traditional appearance of the Hebrew biblical text.

### The Greek Title

The Greek title opens another part of the story.

Several centuries before the beginning of the Common Era, Jewish scholars began translating the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek. The translation of the Torah, including Genesis, is generally placed in the third century BCE and associated with Alexandria in Egypt. Other biblical books were translated over time. The resulting Greek scriptural tradition came to be known as the Septuagint.

### The Seventy-Two Translators

An ancient account known as the *Letter of Aristeas* tells the story of seventy-two Jewish elders, six from each of the twelve tribes of Israel, who were sent from Jerusalem to Egypt during the reign of Ptolemy II Philadelphus. They were charged with translating the Jewish Law into Greek. According to the story, they completed the work in seventy-two days.

The number gave the translation its traditional name. *Septuagint* comes from the Latin word for “seventy,” and the translation is consequently often abbreviated **LXX**, the Roman numeral for seventy.

### The Story Grows in the Telling

The story grew in the telling.

In later versions, the translators were separated so that they could not consult one another. Each produced the translation independently. When their work was compared, their translations agreed perfectly—in some versions, word for word. Their miraculous agreement was taken as evidence that the translation itself had been divinely guided.

It is a wonderful story, but it is a legend. The earliest account of the seventy-two translators does not contain the fully developed miracle of seventy-two independent, identical translations. That feature belongs to the later history of the tradition.

### Why Greek Is on the Cover

The Greek on the cover therefore is not decoration. It recalls an extraordinarily old history of translating Scripture. Jewish readers were translating their Scriptures for Greek-speaking communities more than two thousand years ago. The Septuagint became one of the most influential translations in the history of the Bible.

The Hebrew and Greek titles together place the reader at the meeting point of the biblical text and the long history of reading, transmitting, and translating it.

## How to Read the Right Column

### Read It as a Book

The right column is the World English Bible, or **WEB**.

Read it continuously, just as you would read a book.

**The right column is for reading.**

---

## How to Read the Left Column

The left column invites you to stop and explore, but it does not require you to do so.

### 1. Note the Source

Each illuminated verse identifies whether its English foundation is the **Bible in Basic English (BBE)** or the **World English Bible (WEB)**.

### 2. Read the English

Scan the English in bold. You should be able to read the English naturally without stopping to work through every illumination.

### 3. Follow Your Curiosity

When something catches your attention—or when you are simply curious—read the transliteration. It lets you encounter the sound and form of the original word without requiring you to know the original alphabet.

### 4. Read the IPA

If you can read IPA, read the IPA pronunciation as well. It provides a more precise guide to pronunciation than ordinary English spelling can provide.

### 5. Read the Original Script

If you are studying the Hebrew alphabet, read the Hebrew itself. As the Illuminated Bible moves into other biblical languages, the same invitation applies to their scripts.

### 6. Explore the Footnotes

Read the footnotes when you want to go deeper. They provide room for lexical detail, historical context, cross-references, grammatical observations, and questions of interpretation that would interrupt the verse if placed inline.

### Read as Deeply as You Wish

You do not have to do all of these things.

You may read straight through. You may stop at a single word. You may sound out a transliteration, examine the original script, consult the IPA, follow a footnote, or simply move on.

## The Reading Principle

**The right column is for reading.**

**The left column is for illuminating what catches your attention.**

**Illumination serves the Word. It does not replace it.**
