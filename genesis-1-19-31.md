## 20 — WEB

God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** said, “Let the **waters — מַיִם — mayim — /ˈma.jim/** abound with **living creatures — נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה — nefesh chayah — /ˈne.feʃ χaˈja/**, and let **birds** fly above the **earth — אֶרֶץ — eretz — /ˈe.ʁets/** in the open **expanse — רָקִיעַ — raqia — /ʁaˈki.a/** of the **sky — שָׁמַיִם — shamayim — /ʃaˈma.jim/**.”

### Note — nefesh chayah

**Nefesh chayah** is commonly translated here as “living creature.” *Nefesh* has a much broader biblical range than the English word “creature” suggests and can elsewhere be rendered “being,” “life,” “person,” or “soul.” It is closely associated with living, breathing beings, but *nefesh* does not simply mean “something that breathes.”

Genesis itself provides particularly important cross-references. Genesis 1:24 again uses *nefesh chayah* for land creatures. Genesis 2:7 uses the same expression of the human: after God forms *ha-adam* and breathes into him the breath of life, *ha-adam* becomes a **nefesh chayah**, a “living being.”

The Hebrew connection should be exposed without implying that every occurrence of *nefesh* carries every later theological meaning associated with the English word “soul.”

## 21 — WEB

God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** created the **great sea creatures — הַתַּנִּינִם הַגְּדֹלִים — hattanninim hagedolim**, and every **living creature — נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה — nefesh chayah** that moves, with which the waters abounded, **after their kind**, and every winged bird after its kind. God saw that **it was good — כִּי־טוֹב — ki tov — /ki tov/**.

### Note — tanninim

The Hebrew **tanninim** has a broader biblical range than the English “sea creatures.” Elsewhere *tannin* can designate great serpentine, dragon-like, or formidable creatures. The translation should remain appropriate to the immediate context while the illumination exposes the richer Hebrew vocabulary.

## 22 — Basic English

And God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** gave them his blessing, saying, **“Be fruitful and multiply” — פְּרוּ וּרְבוּ — peru urvu**, and let the waters of the seas be full, and let birds be increased on the earth — **אֶרֶץ — eretz — /ˈe.ʁets/**.

### Note — peru urvu

**Peru urvu** is a compact two-part formula.

*Peru* comes from the root **parah**, meaning “to bear fruit” or “be fruitful.” The biological metaphor preserved in English “fruitful” is already present in the Hebrew.

*Urvu* comes from **ravah**, meaning “to become many,” “increase,” or “multiply.”

The verbs therefore complement rather than merely duplicate one another: **bear fruit; become many**.

The same formula will recur in Genesis 1:28 when the blessing is addressed to humanity.

## 24 — WEB

God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** said, “Let the **earth — אֶרֶץ — eretz — /ˈe.ʁets/** produce **living creatures — נֶפֶשׁ חַיָּה — nefesh chayah** after their kind, **livestock / beasts — בְּהֵמָה — behemah**, **creeping things — רֶמֶשׂ — remes**, and animals of the earth after their kind”; and it was so.

### Note — behemah and Behemoth

**Behemah** is broader than the modern English agricultural category “livestock.” Depending upon context, it can denote an animal or beast, often particularly a larger quadruped and frequently a domesticated animal.

The word is directly related to **Behemoth — בְּהֵמוֹת — behemot**, the extraordinary creature described in Job 40:15.

English subsequently borrowed biblical **Behemoth** as **behemoth**, now used for anything extraordinarily large, massive, formidable, or powerful.

English words that descend from or are directly influenced by illuminated Hebrew vocabulary should be pointed out where the relationship genuinely helps the reader understand or remember the Hebrew.

See also the *nefesh chayah* note at Genesis 1:20.

## 26 — Basic English

God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** said, **“Let us make — נַעֲשֶׂה — na'aseh** **man / humankind — אָדָם — adam** **in our image — בְּצַלְמֵנוּ — betzalmenu**, **like us / according to our likeness — כִּדְמוּתֵנוּ — kidmutenu**”; and let them have rule over the creatures of the sea, the birds of the sky, the animals, the earth, and the creatures moving on the earth.

### Note — “Let us make … our image … our likeness”

The Hebrew plurals are real. **Na'aseh** means “let us make”; **betzalmenu**, “in our image”; and **kidmutenu**, “according to our likeness.”

The grammar therefore raises the question: **to whom does the “us” refer?**

Interpretations have included God addressing a heavenly or divine council, and Christian readers have historically perceived Trinitarian significance in the wording. The grammar itself does not determine among theological or historical interpretations.

Verse 27 makes the question still more striking because the language returns to the singular: **Elohim created** the human **in his image**.

This is an example of the governing principle:

**Illumination may raise questions the text itself does not answer.**

### Cross-reference — adam

Here **adam** can mean “human” or “humankind”; it need not yet function as the personal name Adam.

Compare Genesis 2:7, where **ha-adam**, “the human,” is formed from **ha-adamah**, “the ground.” The fuller *adam–adamah* wordplay should be illuminated there.

## 27 — Basic English

So **God — אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** **created — בָּרָא — bara — /baˈʁa/** **the human — הָאָדָם — ha-adam** **in his image — בְּצַלְמוֹ — betzalmo**; **in the image of God — בְּצֶלֶם אֱלֹהִים — betzelem Elohim** he created him; **male and female he created them — זָכָר וּנְקֵבָה בָּרָא אֹתָם — zakhar u-neqevah bara otam**.

### Note — singular and plural

Verse 26 says:

**“Let us make … in our image … according to our likeness.”**

Verse 27 then returns to singular grammar:

**“Elohim created … in his image.”**

A second grammatical movement occurs within verse 27 itself. The text speaks first of **ha-adam**, “the human,” in the singular, and concludes:

**zakhar u-neqevah bara otam** — “male and female he created them.”

The illumination exposes these movements without requiring the reader to adopt a particular interpretation of them.

## 28 — Basic English

God — **אֱלֹהִים — Elohim — /e.loˈhim/** gave them his blessing and said:

**Be fruitful — פְּרוּ — peru**  

**and multiply — וּרְבוּ — urvu**  

**fill — מִלְאוּ — mil'u** the earth,  

**subdue it — כִבְשֻׁהָ — kivshuha**,  

and **have dominion / rule — רְדוּ — redu** over the creatures of the sea, the birds of the sky, and every living thing moving on the earth.

### Note — kavash and radah

The Hebrew verbs translated “subdue” and “have dominion” are strong words.

**Kavash** means to subdue, subject, or bring under control. The force of “subdue” is therefore present in the Hebrew and is not merely an embellishment introduced by English translation.

**Radah** means to rule, govern, or exercise dominion over.

The lexical meanings should be presented before modern interpretations are imposed upon them. The words themselves neither require an exploitative interpretation nor erase the strength of the authority expressed.

Compare **peru urvu** in Genesis 1:22, where the same “be fruitful and multiply” formula is addressed to the animals.

**Okhlah**, “food,” belongs to the same word family as **akhal — אָכַל**, “to eat.”

The relationship can be shown inline without requiring a separate extended footnote.

The vegetation and seed vocabulary deliberately recalls Genesis 1:11–12.

# Editorial Rules Established in Genesis 1:19–31

## English foundation

The **Bible in Basic English** is the normal English foundation of *The Illuminated Bible*. Its deliberately simple language leaves room for the original language to do the illuminating.

The **World English Bible** is used selectively where its phrasing is clearer, more graceful, or better preserves a feature important to the original text.

**Basic English is therefore the foundation; the WEB appears when it earns its place.**

In working conversation, these may simply be called **Basic English** and **the WEB**.

## English descendants of Hebrew

When an illuminated Hebrew word has produced or directly influenced a recognizable English word, the connection should be identified when it genuinely illuminates the Hebrew.

Example:

**behemah → Behemoth → English “behemoth.”**

## Cross-references rather than repetition

Once a substantial lexical note has been established, later occurrences should normally cross-reference that note rather than reproduce it in full.

## Questions raised by grammar

When Hebrew grammar exposes a significant question, the illumination should make the grammatical evidence visible before presenting interpretations.

**Illumination may raise questions the text itself does not answer.**
